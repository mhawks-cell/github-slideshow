Most PMOs are set up with good intentions and abandoned within two years. They start as a mandate from leadership, generate a burst of activity — templates, governance docs, status dashboards — and then slowly fade into irrelevance as project teams route around them.

Here's what separates the PMOs that stick from the ones that collect dust.

## The Fatal Mistake: Starting With Process

The most common PMO failure mode is leading with methodology. An enthusiastic PMO director spends months crafting the Perfect Process Framework™ — then rolls it out to project managers who are already juggling five initiatives and see the new paperwork as pure overhead.

**The fix:** Start with a problem that matters. What's the #1 thing making projects painful right now? Start there. Process follows value.

## Three Foundations of a Sticky PMO

### 1. Serve the Project Managers, Not the Executives

A PMO that exists primarily to feed executive dashboards will be resented by the people doing the work. Design your PMO so that PMs actually get something out of it — templates that save time, escalation paths that unblock them, resources they can access.

When PMs feel the PMO helps them succeed, they'll feed it accurate data. When they feel it's surveillance, they'll game it.

### 2. Right-Size the Governance

Not every project needs the same level of oversight. A $50K internal tool project and a $5M system integration have very different risk profiles. Implement tiered governance — light touch for small projects, full rigor for large ones.

A simple tiering model based on budget, risk, and strategic impact goes a long way toward reducing compliance burden while maintaining control where it matters.

### 3. Measure Outcomes, Not Compliance

If your PMO's KPIs are "templates submitted" and "status reports filed," you're measuring the wrong things. Track delivery outcomes: on-time delivery rate, budget variance, stakeholder satisfaction, and benefits realization.

Outcome metrics hold the PMO accountable for actual results, not just process adherence.

## The Long Game

A PMO earns its keep by making projects more likely to succeed. That sounds obvious, but it's easy to lose sight of when you're deep in governance design. Every process, template, and review meeting should pass the test: does this make delivery better or worse?
